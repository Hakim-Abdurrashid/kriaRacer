THE SHORT VERSION

Explaining it in one minute

“Our car is reactive. A Hokuyo lidar measures 1,081 distances across the front 270° of the car, 40 times a second. For each scan, our planner on the KR260 finds the most open 50° slice of track, steers half the angle toward it, and picks its speed from the clear distance straight ahead: 2 m/s with 1 m of room, rising to 5 m/s with 4 m or more. That takes about 1.5 ms.

Every scan, an emergency brake checks the path the car is about to drive and stops it if it would hit something within 0.12 s. The planner is our team's follow-the-gap, ported line by line to C++ and checked against the original on 5,000 recorded scans.

The KR260's FPGA runs a YOLO car detector on the camera at 30 frames per second, on hardware the driving loop doesn't use. Today the detector only logs other cars; using it to follow and pass is the next step.”

Two things people often get wrong, so say them precisely. The FPGA does not drive the car: the Arm cores do. And the car does not use a map or a racing line in the race: it reacts to each scan.

The planner, step by step

  1. Read. 1,081 distances, one every 0.25° across 270°, 40 times a second. Anything farther than 10 m counts as 10 m.
  2. Smooth. Average each distance with its two neighbours, so one stray reading can't open or close a gap.
  3. Bubble. Find the closest reading and ignore 175 beams (43.75°) on each side of it.
  4. Gap. Keep the longest run of beams that isn't ignored.
  5. Aim. Slide a 200-beam (50°) window across the gap, average the distances in it, and aim at the centre of the window with the most room.
  6. Steer. Steering angle = half the aim angle, limited to ±0.50 rad (28.6°).
  7. Speed. Average the distance straight ahead (±5°): 1 m or less gives 2.0 m/s, 4 m or more gives 5.0 m/s, in a straight line between. Then multiply by the speed scale (×1.0 or ×1.1).
  8. Brake check. Project the car's path for that steering. If a reading in the car's width along it is less than 0.12 s away, command zero speed for 0.4 s.

What the bubble lands on: our own car

The lidar sees a small part of our own car, just behind and to the side of the sensor. Because it is always the closest thing in the scan, the bubble lands there, not on a wall.

11/07 practice: 91% of scans (−127°, 11 cm) 11/06 run 6: 98% 11/06 run 5: 100%

The rest are readings within 11 cm of the sensor, which no wall gets near while driving. So in practice the car is steered by step 5: it aims at the 50° slice of track with the most room, which tends to point down the track and around corners. That is why it drives smoothly and doesn't flinch at one close reading.

For the 1v1 this matters. A car directly ahead shortens the clear distance, so we slow down, to 2.0 m/s at 1 m. A car beside us only makes that side look less open. The emergency brake is the last resort.

How it drives a lap

Speed comes only from the clear distance straight ahead; steering only from the most open direction. Together they make the car brake for corners on its own: as the wall at the end of a straight comes up, the clear distance shrinks and the speed drops. 04

What keeps it safe

Deadman button

The car drives itself only while RB is held. Let go and race_core commands zero speed on the next scan; a joystick message older than 0.3 s counts as let go.

Emergency brake

Every scan, race_core projects the path for the current steering and looks for readings inside the car's width (0.17 m each side) ahead of the front bumper. If one is less than 0.12 s away at the current speed, it commands zero for 0.4 s. It is off below 0.8 m/s. 0.12 s is about 0.6 m at 5 m/s.

Watchdogs

No lidar scan for 0.15 s: stop. The joystick driver runs in a restart loop, because it crashed once when the pad woke up from sleep.

Hand control

Hold LB to drive by hand at any time; the mux gives the pad priority. Press the pad's Home button before each run, because it sleeps after a few idle minutes.

05

Where the KR260 fits

Most of our lap time comes from the planner and its tuning, and that software would run on any of the computers in this field. What the KR260 adds is room: it runs camera perception on separate hardware, at full frame rate, while the driving loop stays light and on its own cores. None of the other cars in this race carried a camera, so that capability was ours alone.

Not used yet

  • Detections don't steer or brake. They are logged with a bearing to each car. Following and passing with them needs testing against a second car.
  • The two Cortex-R5F real-time cores are idle.
  • Nothing in the FPGA touches the lidar. The planner takes 1.5 ms on the Arm cores, so it doesn't need to.

Fair comparison with a Jetson

On paper a Jetson has faster Arm cores and a GPU that could also run a detector. Our case is not raw speed. It is that perception runs on dedicated hardware with steady timing, next to a driving loop that uses a small slice of the CPU, on the only car in the race with a camera.

REFERENCE

Glossary

Lidar scan
One sweep of the laser: 1,081 distances, each tagged with its angle.
Follow-the-gap
A reactive planner: steer toward the most open part of the scan. No map, no memory between scans.
Bubble
Beams around the closest reading that the planner ignores, meant to keep it away from obstacles.
Bearing / aim
The angle of the chosen direction, 0 = straight ahead, positive = left.
Speed scale (×1.0, ×1.1)
One number that multiplies every speed the planner picks, changeable while driving.
TTC
Time to collision: distance along the path divided by speed.
EKF
Extended Kalman filter. Blends the gyro and wheel speed into position, heading and speed.
DPU
AMD's deep-learning processor, built in the FPGA fabric. Runs our YOLO detector.
FPGA fabric
Programmable logic on the KR260's chip, configured here as the DPU.
race_core
Our C++ driving program: planner, speed rule, emergency brake, deadman, lap counter.
Deadman
A button that must be held for the car to move (RB).
ROS 2 topic
A named data stream between programs, such as /scan or /drive.
